# Separating bulk data from control traffic

A button press has to reach the robot within tens of milliseconds. A video
frame can be a frame late and nobody notices. When both travel through the
same queue, the frames win, because there are more of them.

The GUI bridge showed the failure. After half a minute of streaming drink
detection, a press of **Confirm** took several seconds to reach the arm. The
bridge was sending about 30 Remote Control calls a second to Unreal over the
same websocket Unreal used to deliver button events. Unreal serves one
connection's messages in order, roughly one per rendered frame, so the press
queued behind hundreds of unanswered calls.

This page is the convention for keeping control traffic out from behind bulk
data.

## The convention

Latency-sensitive messages get their own channel. Bulk data is sent only when
it changed, only when someone can see it, and only at the size the consumer
needs.

**Separate channels.** The bridge opens two websockets to Unreal: a
receive-only connection that registers the preset and delivers `UserInput`
events, and a command connection for calls and their responses. A press never
waits behind a call.

**Bulk data on change, sized for the consumer.**

- The bridge sends joint arrays to Unreal only when a joint moved, and the
  system state only when it changed, each with a 2 s refresh. Idle traffic to
  the GUI fell from 40 KB/s to 5 KB/s, and the GUI's own CPU fell with it.
- The drink node used to stream its segmentation mask to the GUI on every
  frame, and the GUI could not keep up. It now sends the bounding box, which
  is what the operator looks at.
- Rendering has a budget as well. Unreal uncapped used two cores and the whole
  GPU to draw a mostly static screen. Capped at 25 fps it draws the same 15 Hz
  camera feeds at a third of the cost.

**One process per kind of traffic.** The GIL makes a Python process that
copies 35 MB/s of video into a socket a poor place to also answer a service
call. When a node has to do both, the video path belongs in its own process,
or at least its own thread with the work done in C (a `cv2` call, a socket
send) so the GIL is released.

**Bound the worst case, not the average.** Cup detection ran DBSCAN over every
valid depth pixel. A typical frame took a second; a close-up of the cup took
18 seconds, during which the node answered nothing. Grid-subsampling above
4,000 points brought the worst frame to 330 ms with poses identical to
0.2 mm. When a per-frame algorithm's cost depends on the input, test it on
the largest input the camera can produce, and keep a recorded bag of that
input in the repo so the check can be repeated.

<Callout type="info">
  Do the structural fix first, then trim the volume. Splitting the channel
  removed the lag by itself; sending less is what keeps it from coming back.
</Callout>

## A module that does this

The GUI bridge in Demo-Software (`core/rammp_prototype_gui`):

- A second websocket for `UserInput` events.
- Calls to Unreal sent on change, with a periodic refresh.
- Video frames forwarded as they arrive from the cameras, on their own path.

The press that took 5 to 8 seconds now arrives in 11 ms. See
[Demo-Software #258](https://github.com/rammp-org/Demo-Software/issues/258) for
the measurements and
[#259](https://github.com/rammp-org/Demo-Software/pull/259) for the change.

## What does not belong here

- **A higher priority for the control path.** Priorities do not help when both
  paths share one queue in another process.
- **A bigger queue.** It moves the lag; it does not remove it.
- **Compression of the bulk stream on the thread that handles control.** The
  compression is then what the press waits behind.
