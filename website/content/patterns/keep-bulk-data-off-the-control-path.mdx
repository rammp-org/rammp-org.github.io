# Keep bulk data off the control path

A button press has to reach the robot in tens of milliseconds. A video frame
can be late by a frame and nobody notices. When both travel through the same
queue, the frames win, because there are more of them.

The GUI bridge showed this in September 2026: once drink detection had streamed
for half a minute, pressing **Confirm** took 5 to 8 seconds to reach the arm.
The bridge sent about 30 Remote Control calls a second to Unreal over the same
websocket that Unreal used to deliver button events, Unreal serves one
connection's messages in order at roughly one per frame, and the press queued
behind hundreds of unanswered calls.

## The convention

**Give latency-sensitive messages their own channel.** The bridge now opens two
websockets to Unreal: a receive-only connection that registers the preset and
delivers `UserInput` events, and a command connection for calls and their
responses. A press can no longer wait behind a call. The same press now arrives
in 11 ms.

**Send bulk data only on change, only when someone can see it, and sized for
the consumer.**

- The bridge sends joint arrays to Unreal only when a joint moved, and the
  system state only when it changed, each with a 2 s refresh. Idle traffic to
  the GUI fell from 40 KB/s to 5 KB/s, and the GUI's own CPU fell with it.
- The drink node used to stream its segmentation mask to the GUI on every frame.
  The GUI could not keep up and grew sluggish. It now sends the bounding box,
  which is what the operator looks at.
- Rendering has a budget too. Unreal uncapped used two cores and the whole GPU
  to draw a mostly static screen; capped at 25 fps it draws the same 15 Hz
  camera feeds at a third of the cost.

**Do not pump bulk data and serve latency from one Python process.** The GIL
makes a process that copies 35 MB/s of video into a socket a poor place to
also handle a service call. When a node has to do both, the video path belongs
in its own process, or at least its own thread with the work done in C (a
`cv2` call, a socket send) so the GIL is released.

<Callout type="info">
  The order of fixes matters. Splitting the channel fixed the 5 s lag by
  itself; sending less made it robust. Do the structural fix first, then trim
  the volume.
</Callout>

**Bound the worst case, not the average.** Cup detection ran DBSCAN over every
valid depth pixel. On a typical frame that was a second; on a close-up of the
cup it was 18 seconds, during which the node answered nothing. Grid-subsampling
above 4,000 points made the worst frame 330 ms with poses identical to 0.2 mm.
When a per-frame algorithm's cost depends on the input, test it on the largest
input the camera can produce, and keep a recorded bag of that input in the repo
so the check can be repeated.

## What does not belong here

- **Raising the priority of the control path.** Priorities do not help when
  both paths share one queue in another process.
- **A bigger queue.** It moves the lag, it does not remove it.
- **Compressing the bulk stream on the same thread** that handles control. The
  compression is now the thing the press waits behind.

## A module that does this

The GUI bridge in Demo-Software (`core/rammp_prototype_gui`): a second websocket
for events, calls sent on change with a periodic refresh, and video frames
forwarded as they arrive from the cameras. See
[Demo-Software #258](https://github.com/rammp-org/Demo-Software/issues/258) for
the measurements and
[#259](https://github.com/rammp-org/Demo-Software/pull/259) for the change.
